03 / Three accountabilities

Different contributions.
One Scrum Team.

A Scrum Team is a cohesive unit of professionals (typically 10 or fewer people) with no sub-teams or hierarchies, focused on one Product Goal at a time.

PO · VALUE & DIRECTION

Product Owner

Maximizes the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team.

  • Product Goal: Develops and explicitly communicates the long-term objective.
  • Product Backlog: Creates, orders and clearly communicates items so the team understands what matters most.
  • Stakeholder alignment: Represents the needs of customers and stakeholders, saying “no” to low-value work.
  • Single decision-maker: One person (not a committee) holds final accountability for backlog ordering.
SM · EFFECTIVENESS & FLOW

Scrum Master

Establishes Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide and is accountable for the Scrum Team’s effectiveness.

  • Coaches the team: Helps members in self-management, cross-functionality and focus on high-value Increments.
  • Removes impediments: Causes the removal of blockers and distractions that slow down progress.
  • Supports the PO: Helps find techniques for effective Product Goal definition and backlog management.
  • Serves the organization: Facilitates stakeholder collaboration and empirical planning across teams.
DEV · QUALITY & DELIVERY

Developers

The people in the Scrum Team committed to creating any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint.

  • Sprint Backlog: Create the actionable plan for the Sprint and forecast how much work they can complete.
  • Definition of Done: Instill quality by adhering to shared quality standards.
  • Daily adaptation: Inspect progress daily and adapt their plan toward the Sprint Goal.
  • Mutual accountability: Hold each other accountable as professionals delivering together.
Does “Developer” mean software programmer?

No. In Scrum, anyone creating the Increment is called a Developer — including designers, writers, analysts, researchers, testers, engineers and operations specialists. The team is cross-functional, meaning its members have all the skills necessary to create value each Sprint.

Who decides who does what, when and how? Where is the Project Manager?

The Scrum Team is self-managing: they internally decide who does what, when and how. Traditional project management responsibilities are distributed across the three accountabilities (PO owns value and scope priorities, Developers own estimation and daily task planning, SM owns process effectiveness). There is no separate “Project Manager” or “Team Lead” assigning individual tasks.

How do Stakeholders fit in, and what are common role pitfalls?
  • Stakeholders (users, customers, sponsors, other teams): They are not part of the Scrum Team, but they collaborate closely during Sprint Review and provide crucial feedback to the Product Owner.
  • PO pitfall — “Order Taker” or “Micromanager”: Simply copying stakeholder requests without prioritizing value, or dictating technical tasks to Developers.
  • SM pitfall — “Team Secretary” or “Scrum Police”: Only booking meetings and moving Jira tickets, or enforcing rigid rules without helping the team solve real problems.
  • Developer pitfall — “Siloed Worker”: Focusing only on individual subtasks (“my part is done”) while the shared Sprint Goal remains unfinished.

05 / Scrum events

The right people.
A clear purpose.

Sprint: the container for all four meetings below; one month or less. The whole Scrum Team works toward the Sprint Goal.

“Owner” is a practical shorthand. Scrum does not appoint one meeting owner for every event. The Scrum Master ensures that events happen and are productive; this does not mean chairing every meeting.

Backlog refinement — who owns it and who joins?

The Product Owner is accountable for effective Product Backlog management. Developers contribute clarification and sizing; relevant experts may help. Refinement is an ongoing activity, not a formal Scrum event. A scheduled refinement meeting is an optional team practice.

Official timeboxes

Daily Scrum: 15 minutes. For a one-month Sprint: Planning up to 8 hours, Review up to 4 hours, Retrospective up to 3 hours. These three events are usually shorter for shorter Sprints.

Facilitator · 15–21
Distinguish accountability from facilitation. The Scrum Master does not need to chair every event; Developers conduct the Daily Scrum. Review includes stakeholders; Retrospective focuses on the Scrum Team. Source: Scrum Guide, Scrum Events and Scrum Master sections.

How to run the exercise in Jira
  1. Before the session, prepare a training project/space or agreed training area with one board or team filter per crew. Add the links in Setup.
  2. Create one issue named “First-Day Survival Guide” plus the eight backlog tasks below. Use “Copy issue text” or import the team CSV. Select a suitable Task work type and map Summary, Description and Labels; your Jira permissions and configuration determine import availability.
  3. Record the Sprint Goal in the exercise Sprint Goal field, or in the guide issue when using a board without sprints. Select work and plan it together.
  4. Move selected work through your Jira workflow (for example To do → In progress → Done). Check acceptance conditions and the Definition of Done before marking work complete.
  5. Keep the usable guide in the guide issue description. Capture user feedback in comments and a teamwork improvement as a follow-up task. Use the second round to improve it.

CSV export creates a file for manual import. It does not create issues or start a sprint. Use your existing workflow; Jira labels and statuses do not define Scrum.

Live Q&A parking lot

Frequently asked questions

What happens if we don’t finish a Sprint Backlog item by the end of the Sprint?

We do not extend the Sprint, and we do not count unfinished work as part of the Increment. The item returns to the Product Backlog, where the Product Owner re-orders it based on current priorities. In the Sprint Retrospective, the team inspects why it happened (e.g., item too large, hidden blockers, over-commitment) and adapts for the next Sprint.

Can stakeholders add new urgent tasks in the middle of a Sprint?

No changes are made that would endanger the Sprint Goal. However, scope may be clarified and renegotiated between the Product Owner and Developers as more is learned. If a genuine production crisis occurs, the team collaborates with the PO to swap out lower-priority work or, in extreme cases where the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete, the PO can cancel the Sprint.

How do we handle unplanned support, bugs or maintenance work in Scrum?

Teams typically handle unplanned operational work in one of two ways: (1) Leave buffer capacity during Sprint Planning based on historical averages so the Sprint Goal remains realistic, or (2) Make visible support/bug-fix items part of the Product Backlog and refine/prioritize them every Sprint alongside feature work.

How does Scrum work when stakeholders ask for fixed deadlines or long-term roadmaps?

Scrum uses empiricism (actual completed Increments over past Sprints) to forecast future delivery rather than relying on upfront guesswork. By delivering a usable Increment every Sprint, stakeholders get early visibility into real progress and can adjust scope so the highest-value features are always delivered first by the target date.
